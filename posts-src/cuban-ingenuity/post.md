Q: I. Curatorial Statement

With this exhibit, it is our sincere desire to pay tribute to the bravery and intelligence of the Cuban people. Bravery in facing circumstances from difficult to dire. And intelligence in crafting endless ingenious ways to meet and overcome the challenges of those circumstances.

All objects in the exhibition were part of everyday lives of ordinary Cubans. They were acquired in situ, directly from the people who invented or used them. In many cases, we gave the owners new replacement tools or items in trade for what they allowed us to collect for this exhibit. The exhibit is accompanied by video that reveals the beauty of Cuba – from the beaches to the countryside to the architecture of the cities. The music and language in the documentary convey the tempo/rhythm of Cuba's people and its streets. The documentary allows the viewer a glimpse of the context of these objects in use.

^Documentary "Cuban Ingenuity" by Jorge Lavoy

While we focus on recognizing the grit of Cubans in facing reality, we also wish to recognize their dreams and hopes. The yearning to fly and the urge to cross the borders of the island are referred to in the four images of the logo – the ship, the submarine, the airplane, and the hot air balloon. We are pleased to complement the Cuban Ingenuity exhibit with art, the sculpture Hybrid of a Chrysler (2016) by Esterio Segura. The vintage car with wings is a symbol of the dreams to break free from the confines of an island, and a salute to the creativity and ingenuity of the people of Cuba.

Q: II. Introduction Panel

Cubans prevail over a grim reality of need with both ingenuity and humor. The necessity of repurposing objects and materials is fundamental to daily life in Cuba. Sometimes ingenious, sometimes outrageous, Cuban 'inventions' – often described as 'Criollo' – served as both functional solutions and creative achievements.

In 1961, when diplomatic relations between the US and Cuba were severed, Cuban factories were largely equipped with American-made machinery. A US trade embargo disallowed the import of needed replacement parts. The widespread emigration of engineers out of Cuba compounded the difficulty of keeping industry alive. The Minister of Industries, Che Guevara, began a social campaign encouraging the ingenuity of factory workers to keep machinery functioning and factories productive. Over time, national pride in the triumph of inventiveness extended from factory workers to the Cuban people at large.

^The many uses of the Aurika Motor in Cuba

In the 1990s, during the Special Period in the Time of Peace – the economic crisis that began with the collapse of the Soviet Union and led to a period of great deprivation for the Cuban people – inventiveness and ingenuity were essential for survival. Ingenuity in making do with what is available was woven into the fabric of daily life and fixed in the identity of Cubans. Innovation and resourcefulness is seen in every aspect of life, from food to architecture, from tools to technology. Resources are exploited to their fullest, materials are recycled, objects are repurposed. Nothing is wasted. Lacking inventory, the Cuban people have become inventors.

Cuban Ingenuity – the Artistry of the Everyday Inventor is a selection of inventive objects, collected in Cuba and supported by interviews, video documentary, and commentary to illustrate the ingenuity of the Cuban people.

Q: III. Objects (Selection)

**Modified Soviet Orbita Fan** ca. 1998 — Orbita motor, Moskvitch disk brake, metal water pipe, recycled plastic, repurposed aluminum. For Cubans, the noisy vibrating of the Orbita fan was a soundtrack of daily life. The Orbita is a Soviet fan model exported to Cuba during the 1970s and 1980s. It was often used to defrost refrigerators. Orbita motors are salvaged and applied to uses such as water turbines or knife sharpeners. In this modified Orbita, the base is a Moskvitch brake disc, the stand is a metal water pipe with enough weight to keep the fan from traveling, and the blades are constructed from recycled plastics. Orbitas are still found operating all over Cuba today — an appliance so common it is an icon of everyday life to many Cubans.

**Vertical Power Saw** ca. 2013 — Mid-20th-century sewing machine, Soviet Aurika motor, hacksaw blade, fiberglass. An elegant integration of sewing machine and washing machine motor for jewelry making. Two artisan brothers devised this vertical power saw to cut plastics and wood for handcrafted jewelry, combs, and artisan wares, using the body of their mother's old sewing machine refitted with a hacksaw blade and an Aurika washing machine motor. The resulting contraption is a homemade stationary jigsaw.

**Compressed-Fuel Stove** ca. 1994 — Galvanized zinc, brass, aluminum. Entirely homemade, this cooking stove regulates its flame with air pressure: a manual bicycle air pump puts kerosene in the tank under pressure, and fuel travels to the pilot where combustion takes place. Mainly used outdoors for frying.

**Water Heaters** 1996 / 2012 / 2018 — Wood or recycled plastic, nichrome heater wire, power cord. The lack of consistent running water or water pressure makes a bucket with a submersible water heater the most common and practical way to bathe throughout Cuba. Cubans craft their own from whatever materials are at hand — metal coat hangers, condensed-milk cans, clay, wood, wire — and are quite used to the reality of slight electrical shocks when using them.

**Stove Ignition / Fire Starter** ca. 1995, 2005 — Two versions, both using low-voltage transformers to produce a spark. Gas stoves in Cuba did not come with a built-in starter until only a few years ago, so ignition depended on a spark from an independent starter such as these — critical during periods of acute crisis when matches and fuel-based lighters were unavailable.

**Electric Lawn Mower** ca. 1998 — Aurika motor, Aurika capacitor, toy stroller wheels, repurposed wood, light switch, machete, power cord. In operation for more than 20 years, devised by attaching a classic Aurika washing machine motor to a repurposed wooden board, with blades taken from machetes and wheels from children's toy strollers.

**Wood Burner / Hot-Wire Cutter** ca. 2011 — Krim 218 Russian television transformer, repurposed wood, Soviet switch, fan dial, wire. Functions two ways: as a woodburner (pyrographer) and as a hot-wire glass cutter for producing drinking glasses from bottle tops, built from a transformer salvaged from an old Russian television.

**Fish Tank Bubbler** ca. 2009 — Wood, aluminum, transformer, homemade pump. Homemade, or Criollo, fish tank air pumps are devised from repurposed or reinvented transformers, with an aluminum rod positioned to oscillate and generate air bubbles through a rubber air pump.

The exhibition Cuban Ingenuity - the Artistry of the Everyday Inventor, curated by Gabriela Azcuy, Anne Gilroy and Jorge Lavoy, was held at the Cade Museum for Creativity & Invention from March to December, 2019.
