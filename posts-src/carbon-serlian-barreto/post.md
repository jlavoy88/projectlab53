^Carbón (triptych) from the series "Carbon", 2020. Courtesy of Pan American Art Projects

Serlian Barreto was born in Cuba in 1997, on the outskirts of Havana, in a humble town in Artemisa whose main economic sources are agriculture, the construction materials industry, and charcoal production. Barreto attended the school Amparucha thanks to the insistence of his grandmother, and graduated eventually with honors from the San Alejandro Art Academy. His work has been exhibited in several galleries in Cuba, in the Havana Biennial and in institutions in Naples, Italy, with pieces in the Cisneros Fontanals Art Foundation (CIFO), the Jorge M. Pérez Collection, and El Oficio Magazine's collection.

His mother and grandfather were veterinarians, and Serlian raised all kinds of animals as a child; his grandfather also produced citrus until a pest led the government to replace that crop with charcoal production. Barreto grew up with his grandfather and became thoroughly familiar with converting organic materials into carbon — a process he did not consciously reflect on until he migrated to the United States, where such labor tends to go unnoticed in a consumer society.

^Allá sobre las cumbres from the series "Carbon", 2019.

Serlian Barreto's paintings are a tribute to his grandfather, but also to nature, to the people, and to each entity that remains invisible behind the whirlpool of a globalized world. The artist conceives of the Carbon series as a literary chronicle, where each work resembles a notebook describing the process: from the collection of dry leaves, logs and snails, to the conception in the oven — though the series title is never actually depicted. His aesthetic draws on artists such as David Hockney, Hernan Bas, and Enrique Martínez Celaya; the contemporary use of perspective and brushwork leaves an intentional sense of incompleteness that makes the viewer a participant, filling the sensory gaps.

^Guanahacabibes from the series "Carbon", 2020.

The series includes large-format works like "Sobre la hojarasca" and "Allá sobre las Cumbres," near-life-size, in which Barreto represents himself collecting raw materials. An oversized triptych pays homage to the charcoal burner (both the worker and the birds that share his name), depicted in precarious, hopeless states — the great paradox of the charcoal burners as victims of their own creation.

^Everglades from the series "Carbon", 2020.

Medium-format close-ups describe the process in detail, including "¿Por qué el sombrero de la noche vuela con tantos agujeros?", referencing Pablo Neruda's Book of Questions. The smallest-format paintings, reduced to their minimum expression like charcoal itself, were inspired by a trip Barreto made with his grandfather to the Guanacabibes Nature Reserve on his first return visit after migrating, and later connected to his experience of the Everglades in Miami — a landscape "preserved" but reserved for tourism. In some way, Barreto suggests, we have all become a metaphor for coal, with narrowed capacities because of what silently consumes us from outside.
